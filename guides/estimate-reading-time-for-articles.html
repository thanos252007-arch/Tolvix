How to Estimate Reading Time for Articles and Blog Posts

A reading-time label helps a visitor decide whether to start an article now or save it for later. It is an estimate based mainly on word count, not a stopwatch prediction. Technical tutorials, essays and stories have different reading demands, so the same word count can produce different real reading times.

Choose a sensible reading speed

A frequently used rough estimate for general web reading is around 200–250 words per minute. Treat that as a starting point, not a universal average. Readers may move faster through familiar short updates and slower through dense explanations, unfamiliar terminology or second-language material. If your audience is studying a subject, a lower rate may be more honest.

Calculate the estimate

Count the words in the article body, divide by the selected words-per-minute rate, then round up to a whole minute for a simple label. For example, a 1,000-word article at 220 words per minute takes about 4.55 minutes, commonly displayed as 5 min read. Rounding avoids a false impression that the estimate is exact to the second.

Account for more than words

A word-count calculation does not measure time spent examining diagrams, code blocks, tables, charts, embedded media or interactive exercises. For a hands-on tutorial, consider whether readers need to complete steps as they read. You can add a separate setup or practice-time note when that is useful, rather than inflating the reading-time calculation.

Worked example: estimate a short article

An article with 1,200 words at a chosen speed of 240 words per minute gives an estimate of 5 minutes: 1,200 ÷ 240 = 5. If the article contains code samples, tables or step-by-step tasks, readers may need longer than the arithmetic suggests. Consider whether those elements are central to understanding the article before deciding whether to include them in the count.

Use one method across similar posts so readers can compare estimates. Recalculate after substantial edits, round up consistently when a partial minute remains, and label the result as an estimate rather than a promise about how long every visitor will take.

Where to place the label

Place a short estimate near the article title or byline where it can help visitors choose. Use the same method across comparable articles so labels are consistent. Recalculate after substantial edits, especially when you add or remove examples, instructions or sections.
